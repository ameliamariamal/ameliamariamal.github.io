The Next Generation Impactor

How cascade impaction is used to separate pharmaceutical aerosols according to their aerodynamic particle size.

The Next Generation Impactor (NGI) is a cascade impactor widely used to characterise the aerodynamic particle size distribution (APSD) of aerosols generated from inhaled and nasal medicinal formulations.

Aerodynamic particle size is an important property of pharmaceutical aerosols because it influences how particles behave during transport through the respiratory system and where they may ultimately deposit.

The NGI provides high-resolution separation of an aerosol into different aerodynamic size fractions. Its horizontal configuration also allows convenient access to the individual collection cups, facilitating recovery of deposited material for subsequent gravimetric or chemical analysis.

How is the NGI structured?

The NGI contains seven impaction stages, commonly referred to as Stages 1–7, followed by a micro-orifice collector (MOC).

Depending on the formulation and experimental configuration, the system may also include an induction port and a pre-separator before the aerosol enters the main cascade stages.

Each stage is designed with a characteristic aerodynamic cut-off diameter. As the aerosol passes through successive stages, particles are progressively separated according to their aerodynamic behaviour.

The induction port provides a simplified inlet geometry and can retain a substantial proportion of larger material. However, the NGI does not reproduce the complex anatomical geometry of the human respiratory tract. It is an analytical instrument designed to characterise aerosol performance rather than a complete physiological model of lung deposition.

How does the NGI separate particles?

The NGI operates according to the principle of inertial impaction.

As an aerosol passes through the nozzle arrays of each stage, the airflow is forced to change direction rapidly toward the collection surface.

Particles with sufficient inertia are unable to follow this rapidly changing airflow and instead impact onto the collection surface. Smaller particles have less inertia and are more capable of remaining entrained within the airflow, allowing them to continue toward subsequent stages.

What information can we obtain?

Following an NGI experiment, the quantity of drug or material deposited at each location can be determined. The resulting stage-by-stage distribution provides information about the aerodynamic characteristics of the aerosol.

These data can contribute to parameters such as mass median aerodynamic diameter (MMAD), geometric standard deviation (GSD), fine particle mass (FPM) and fine particle fraction (FPF).

Together, these measurements can be used to compare aerosol formulations, devices and experimental conditions.

Beyond particle size

The NGI provides detailed information about the aerodynamic behaviour of an aerosol. However, knowing where material deposits within an impactor does not by itself explain what happens when that material encounters a biological environment.

Does the deposited material dissolve?
Can the drug cross an epithelial barrier?
Does the formulation influence cell viability or barrier integrity?
Could different aerosol fractions produce different biological responses?

Connecting aerodynamic characterisation with biological analysis may therefore help build a more complete understanding of an inhaled formulation.
